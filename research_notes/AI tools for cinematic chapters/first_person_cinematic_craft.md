## 1. How do shipped first-person games deliver cinematic, story-driven moments without taking the camera away?

### Takeaway
The best-documented case is Valve's Half-Life: Alyx commentary. It shows that "cinematic without cutscenes" is built from many small, cheap tricks: sound that pulls the eye, light and contrast that lead the eye, look-triggers with a timeout fallback, staging spaces so characters and the player stay naturally apart, exposition cut into pieces across the walk, and radio or companion voices that keep the player busy with their hands. Firewatch, Oxenfree and Titanfall 2 apply the same idea to dialogue: talk happens while the player keeps control, through interruptible, restartable, choice-driven conversations. Cairn (the 2025 climbing game) gets much of its drama from systems, such as a fall caused by thirst, rather than from scripted scenes.

### Cited Findings

**Half-Life: Alyx (Valve, 2020): attention direction, from the in-game developer commentary**
- **Look trigger with a timer fallback.** Valve calls this a standard trick: "The combination of a timer and a look trigger is a common trick used in places where we'd like the player to see a neat visual moment." In the shotgun-gate scene the zombies break in when the player looks at the walls *or* when the timer runs out (Dan Ginsburg, "Shotgun Gate 2") — [Combine OverWiki: HL:A developer commentary](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Sound pulls the eye.** In playtests, players often missed the train wreck. Valve added distant train-car noises before the crash and a sharp explosion sound "to draw their attention to the smokestack" (Alireza Razmpoosh, "Train Crash") — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Moving parts that make noise and spark** draw the eye to a critical door. The breaker box and the hole in the wall were placed so the player has a clear line of sight to it (Jason Mitchell, "Critical Junction") — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Light and contrast as a guide.** Environment artists placed a glowing red sign that pulls players toward the goal through visual contrast (Ken Banks, "Tanker Yard Combat") — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Building a reveal with scale references.** Distant structures have no stereo depth, so Valve filled the space between the player and the Citadel with scale references. The eye goes to the Citadel first, then drifts to nearby detail (Tristan Reidford, "Citadel Vista") — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Framing a moment while keeping control.** An elevator door was designed to protect the player while still showing the Strider's performance. Limiting the view from the elevator kept the ride comfortable and still allowed a dramatic reveal (Phil Co; Colby Sieber, "Elevator Comfort") — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **A creature that seems to address the player.** The Strider uses a procedural look-at system, with separate look-ats for body, minigun and cannon, so it seems to be "directly addressing the player" (Brad Kinley) — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Forced gaze during a grab.** When a barnacle grabs the player, its tongue wraps in front of their face wherever they are looking, which guides the eye up to the enemy (Scott Dalton) — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Warning before the event.** "In intense situations, players frequently fail to notice or retain critical information." The Reviver's scream and shadow mark where it is before the key moment (Joe van den Heuvel) — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Staging characters around a player who moves freely.** "We designed these spaces to allow for a natural separation between the player and our characters" (Steve Kalning, "Character Choreography"). A pod slides back so the Vortigaunt comes out "at a comfortable distance," and it climbs on four legs so it doesn't compete with the player for space (Mike Tjosvold) — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Exposition in pieces.** Russell's drone delivered chunks of a long scene across the area before it, so the scene itself could focus on the critical goal (Eddie Parker, "Pacing 2"). Hideout information was split across two rooms, the Vortigaunt's state in one and the new goal in the next (Karen Prell). Larry's exposition about Jeff is spread across several beats (Jamaal Bradley) — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Something to do while the story plays.** Props in the first hideout room give "tinkerers" something to do while the Vortigaunt walks to the next room, so the beat still lands (Matthew Scott). In the Vault panel scene, the radio back-and-forth between Alyx and Russell "keeps players in the moment, flipping levers". The levers only raise tension; they do nothing mechanically (Jeff Leinbaugh) — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Radio voice to point the player's eye.** Russell prompts the player to look out the window at the Vault so they recognise it later (Jason Mitchell) — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Hiding a character to cut animation cost.** The G-Man/collaborator scene is staged so the player overhears without getting a clear look. The track keeps the figure in shadow, which "also reduced animation load and helped the schedule" (Sean Vanaman) — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Fewer reactions felt better.** Too many voiced reactions from Alyx felt off-putting, so Valve kept only the ones nearly every player shared (Jay Pinkerton) — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Cues for players looking elsewhere.** Xen growths react to the same sounds as the blind monster Jeff, so "if players weren't looking at Jeff, they could still be caught unaware" (Simon Fuchs). Barnacles breathe with HRTF-spatialised sound so players hear them overhead (Kelly Cline) — [same source](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Shorter exposition.** Playtesters grew fidgety during long exposition, which led to much shorter expository scenes — [Game Informer interview (2020)](https://www.gameinformer.com/index.php/interview/2020/03/23/valve-talks-half-life-alyx-and-why-arms-dont-work-in-vr); headline confirmed at [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/how-fidgeting-playtesters-convinced-valve-to-drastically-shorten-half-life-alyxs-intro) (body not retrievable)
- **Players lingered longer than planned out of curiosity**, and the team let them — [TechRadar interview](https://www.techradar.com/news/half-life-alyx-dev-talks-vr-the-future-of-the-franchise-and-a-dream-vr-project)
- Search results said a Valve interview treats the "budget of a player's attention" as a central design limit. It is probably the Road to VR interview with Robin Walker, but I did not open it to confirm the wording — [Road to VR](https://roadtovr.com/valve-half-life-alyx-game-design-interview-robin-walker/)

**Valve Source engine tools (Half-Life 2 lineage)**
- Choreographed scenes are stored as VCD (Valve Choreography Data) files, authored in **Faceposer**, and played in the map by a `logic_choreographed_scene` entity. Its keyvalues "Target 1-8" fill in the `!Target#` names used inside the VCD — [VDC: VCD](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Vcd); [VDC: Scripted scene](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Scripted_scene)
- The VCD "Look at Actor" event turns an actor's head toward a target. Targets can be abstract names like `!player`, and "the eyes will always look straight at the target." Example: `lookat "look_player"` with `param "!player"` in `scenes/eli_lab/al_cmon.vcd` — [VDC: Choreography Tool reference](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Choreography_Tool_reference)
- Valve's own wiki pages on Faceposer are acknowledged to be patchy, according to a forum thread surfaced in search — [VDC: Choreography category](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Choreography)

**Firewatch (Campo Santo, 2016): walkie-talkie dialogue**
- GDC 2017 talk "Do You Copy? Dialog System and Tools in Firewatch" (Patrick Ewing, Campo Santo; William Armstrong, Unity). The system started as "an interrupt-heavy bark system" and became the "long, restarting conversations" they shipped. The player can interrupt at any time — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024000/Do-You-Copy-Dialog-System)
- A 2015 interview quotes the team: "We made the dialogue tool in-house, and it syncs with Unity" (from a search snippet; probably the Edge interview) — [Edge via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/australia/edge/20150409/281543699429475)
- How it plays, per reviews: looking at an interactable object or place gives Henry the option to raise the radio and report it to Delilah, or to stay silent. On controller, picking a topic means holding the left shoulder button — [Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/11478-firewatch-showcases-the-power-of-dialogue-in-video-games); [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/?p=114178)

**Oxenfree (Night School, 2016): walk-and-talk**
- Adam Hines (formerly Telltale) wanted an adventure game where you walk and talk at once, inspired by Aaron Sorkin's walk-and-talk scenes. Dialogue choices can be picked at any time: wait, interrupt, or stay silent. There are usually three options — [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/interrupt-dialogue-in-oxenfree-makes-conversations-feel-natural); [MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/business-news/i-thought-we-were-going-to-go-out-of-business-sean-krankel-on-the-development-of-oxenfree)
- Studio head Sean Krankel said they "didn't perfectly manage" how long a walk-and-talk would last. A reviewer noticed that choices are sometimes held until a natural pause and sometimes cut a character off mid-line — [MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/business-news/i-thought-we-were-going-to-go-out-of-business-sean-krankel-on-the-development-of-oxenfree); [Giant Bomb blog](https://www.giantbomb.com/profile/darth_navster/blog/convo-interruptus-how-oxenfree-breaks-new-ground-f/113461/)
- Krankel wanted dialogue to feel like the player's "weapon," since the game has no combat — [PC Gamer: the future of dialogue](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-future-of-dialogue-in-games)

**Titanfall 2 (Respawn, 2016): companion bond without cutscenes**
- Respawn used conversations between pilot and Titan "as a way of strengthening their bond with BT without resorting to cutscenes." The player usually picks between two dialogue options — [TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/?p=272434); [GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/your-robot-buddy-is-the-best-part-of-titanfall-2s-single-player)
- A reported content ratio of "two parts combat, one part platforming, one part puzzling" (2-1-1) — [TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/?p=272434). Respawn's own feature on BT: [EA: Bringing BT-7274 to life](https://www.ea.com/en-au/news/bringing-bt-7274-to-life-in-titanfall-2) (not opened)

**The Last of Us (Naughty Dog, 2013): companion AI**
- Max Dyckhoff's GDC 2014 talk "Ellie: Buddy AI in The Last of Us" covers Ellie's systems and their shortcomings. A summary says Ellie was kept close to Joel so players would not see her as a burden — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/ellie-s-buddy-ai-in-i-the-last-of-us-i-explained-at-gdc-2014); also [Kill Screen](https://www.killscreen.com/how-ellies-pathfinding-made-you-love-her/) and Game AI Pro ch. 35 by Dyckhoff

**Ghost of Tsushima (Sucker Punch, 2020): guidance through the world**
- The Guiding Wind replaces a waypoint marker. The model is simple: mostly one vector pointing from the player to the current goal. The wind simulation favoured "volume over accuracy" (Bill Rockenbeck, GDC 2021 "Blowing from the West") — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1027350/Blowing-from-the-West-Simulating); [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/using-vorticles-to-simulate-wind-in-i-ghost-of-tsushima-i-)
- Parker Hamilton's GDC 2021 talk "Exploration in Ghost of Tsushima: Letting the Island Guide You" covers the natural lures (wind, birds, smoke) that replace HUD markers — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1027399/Exploration-in-Ghost-of-Tsushima); creative director interview on why there are no waypoints: [Android Central](https://www.androidcentral.com/ghost-tsushima-creative-director-interview)

**Cairn (The Game Bakers, 2025): climbing**
- Creative director Emeric Thoa: "There is no human-made animation". Limb placement is inverse kinematics plus procedural animation. "We love video games where gameplay has some kind of meaning... It doesn't have to be a hardcore story, but there is at least meaning that resonates." — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/cairn-s-manual-climbing-system-is-like-a-vertical-death-stranding-)

The reviewer's most dramatic moment was an unscripted fall when Aava collapsed from thirst. It was a systemic event, not a scripted one — [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/cairn-s-manual-climbing-system-is-like-a-vertical-death-stranding-)
- Team of about 25, budget about €5M, development started in 2020 (Wikipedia mirrors; sources disagree on 3 vs 5 years). Worked with mountaineer Elisabeth Revol — [Wikiwand: Cairn](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Cairn_(video_game)); [Button Musher](https://buttonmusher.substack.com/p/how-cairn-got-an-entire-studio-climbing); [Edge interview](https://knowledge.edge-online.com/p/montpellier-rising-the-game-bakers-emeric-thoa-on-cairn-recognition-and-indie-development-in-france)

### Inferences
- **[inference]** Almost every Alyx technique is a *small, reusable component*, not bespoke content: a look-trigger with a timeout, a spatial audio "lure" at a target, an emissive "beacon", a radio line queued on a trigger, a space that keeps a character at a set distance. None needs cutscene tools. One developer with an AI coding agent could build each as a UE component or Blueprint once (a few days each) and then place them across 17 chapters as data.
- **[inference]** For a climbing game this maps directly onto set pieces:
  - *Avalanche*: sound first (Alyx's train wreck pattern), then the visual, triggered by look or timer.
  - *Ghost on a ridge*: a look-trigger in reverse. The figure appears only inside the view cone at long range and fades when the player looks straight at it or brings it up close.
  - *Summit reveal at dusk*: Citadel-vista framing with scale references (prayer flags, other climbers, a ridge line) between the player and the far peaks, plus a light change.
  - *Slip caught by the rope*: Alyx's barnacle pattern. A very short forced reorientation that the player feels as their own body, then control returns at once.
- **[inference]** Cairn and PEAK both show that in climbing games the most memorable "cinematic" moments can come from systems: fatigue, thirst, falls, the rope. Yggdrasil's Peak already has ropes, body effects and weather systems (per project memory). That argues for spending authored-content time on *framing* systemic events (a radio voice reacting, a camera hold, a sound) rather than on fully scripted scenes.
- **[inference]** Firewatch, Oxenfree and Titanfall 2 point to one reusable "conversation while moving" system: interruptible, restartable, with choices offered in a time window. It would cover the radio voices (Base Camp doctor), companions on the rope (Nima) and inner monologue in every chapter.
- **[opinion, mine]** The Alyx note about trimming reactions to "only the ones nearly every player shared" is a useful rule against over-writing companion barks.

## 2. Which first-person cinematic techniques exist, and which can one developer build as reusable UE 5.8 systems?

### Takeaway
The documented toolkit is: look-triggers with timeout, audio lures, light and contrast beacons, framing through geometry (doors, windows, ledges), composing reveals with scale references, procedural look-at on characters, a short forced gaze during a physical event, guidance through world effects (Ghost of Tsushima's wind), radio voices that point the player's eye, and exposition spread across space. On the engine side, UE's Gameplay Cameras plugin is still experimental, and blending between Sequencer cameras and gameplay cameras has known pitfalls. A light custom "camera director" component on the player camera (soft look-at, FOV pulse, short additive camera animations) is therefore the safer bet.

### Cited Findings
- **Look-trigger plus timer** is a standard Valve technique for making sure the player sees a moment — [HL:A commentary, Dan Ginsburg](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Audio lure** (distant sound, then a sharp sound) used to fix a moment players missed in playtests — [HL:A commentary, Alireza Razmpoosh](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Light and contrast beacon** (red emissive sign) for the golden path — [HL:A commentary, Ken Banks](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Darkness as a gate.** Players tried muzzle flashes and UI light to see in the dark, so Valve removed those light sources and used dialogue to point to the real solution — [HL:A commentary, Phil Co](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Music to focus the player.** Simple action music signalled rising threat and kept players from getting distracted by props while heading to safety (Mike Morasky) — [HL:A commentary](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **Head and eye look-at to the player** as an authored event: Source's VCD "Look at Actor" with `!player` — [VDC](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Choreography_Tool_reference). Procedural multi-part look-at on the Strider — [HL:A commentary](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)
- **World-effect guidance** in place of HUD markers: wind as a vector from player to goal — [GDC Vault (Rockenbeck)](https://gdcvault.com/play/1027350/Blowing-from-the-West-Simulating)
- **Body as the emotional channel in first and third person climbing**: breath, grunts, limb tremor, rumble — [Game Developer on Cairn](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/cairn-s-manual-climbing-system-is-like-a-vertical-death-stranding-)
- **UE Gameplay Cameras plugin**: Epic's Quick Start builds First Person, Third Person and Top Down rigs in a Camera Director asset, with transitions between them — [Epic docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/gameplay-camera-system-quick-start). In 5.6 it was still Experimental, with "no proper documentation" beyond the Quick Start (Epic staff on the forum), and camera activation changed between 5.5 and 5.6 — [UE forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/gameplay-camera-rig-broken-in-5-6/2550183/3). A third-party blog expects Beta around 5.8 (unconfirmed) — [StraySpark blog](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/gameplay-cameras-plugin-ue5-7-production-guide)
- **Sequencer to gameplay camera blend pitfalls**: a visible pop at blend start, traced to the aspect-ratio axis constraint; the fix is "Override Aspect Ratio Axis Constraint" on both cameras — [UE forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/blending-from-level-sequence-to-gameplay-camera-causes-instant-pop-despite-matching-settings/2686528). One developer could not drive Gameplay Cameras parameters from Sequencer and switched to standard Cine Cameras, which blended FOV and focal length correctly — [UE forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/running-in-to-issues-blending-between-gameplay-cameras-plugin-cameras-and-regular-camera-components-is-there-a-recommended-workflow-for-this/2708784). Another reports the first-person camera stuck behind the head after a sequence blend-in — [UE forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/first-person-camera-stuck-behined-players-head-after-camera-blend-in-using-a-sequence/2435129)

### Inferences
- **[inference] A reusable "Attention Director" for UE (one C++ actor component on the player camera manager, about 3 to 5 days with an AI coding agent):**
  1. `SoftLookAt(Target, MaxDegrees, Strength, Duration)`: adds a capped yaw/pitch offset toward the target and gives way at once to mouse or stick input above a threshold. The player never loses control. It is a nudge, not a takeover.
  2. `FOVPulse(DeltaDeg, In, Hold, Out)` and `TimeDilation(Scale, Duration)` for the rope-catch and avalanche moments (use the global time dilation already in UE; keep scale ≥0.3 and duration short **[opinion]**).
  3. `PlayCameraAnim(AdditiveCurveAsset)`: short additive camera curves, for example a 0.6 s jolt for a slip caught by the rope. Apply them on top of the player's view rather than swapping to a Sequencer camera. That avoids the blend pops reported above.
  4. `DOFFocusOn(Target)` through the post-process component, for the ghost or the summit.
  5. `LookTrigger` component: view-cone test (dot product against angle plus distance plus line-of-sight trace) with an optional timeout fallback, as in Alyx. It fires a beat event either way.
  6. `AudioLure`: spawns a spatial one-shot at the target, for example a cracking serac, a distant voice or a prayer-flag flutter, to turn the head before a reveal.
  7. `Beacon`: an emissive pulse or light on the target, such as a headlamp in the distance, a red tent or the sun breaking through.
- **[inference]** Because all seven are data-driven (parameters set on a beat row, see section 3), each of the 17 chapters places existing pieces instead of building new ones. The UE camera stack (PlayerCameraManager, CameraModifier, post-process) is mature. Avoiding the experimental Gameplay Cameras plugin and full Sequencer camera cuts sidesteps the forum-reported 5.6 issues.
- **[inference]** Where a true camera takeover is unavoidable (for example a fall into a crevasse), keep it under about 1 to 2 seconds, show the player's own hands and legs (the project already has a visible first-person body and ice-axe IK), and return control on the first input. This follows the Cairn "body sells it" finding and the Alyx barnacle pattern. **[opinion]**

### Gaps
- No primary source found on measured motion-sickness limits for camera nudges or FOV changes in flat-screen first person. The Alyx comfort notes are VR-specific.
- Not verified whether UE 5.8 changed the Gameplay Cameras plugin status. The only claim found is a third-party blog prediction.
- No sourced data on how often shipped games use slow motion or FOV pulses for scripted moments. This is general practice, not documented here.

## 3. What production methods let a tiny team make many such moments quickly?

### Takeaway
Three proven patterns make content cheap per unit:
1. **Rule-matched dialogue ("dynamic dialog").** Valve's system, used in all its games since The Orange Box: hundreds of world facts are fuzzy-matched against thousands of lines, and the most specific rule wins.
2. **Storylets and salience-based selection** (Failbetter, Emily Short). Small, self-contained content units unlocked by state, which recombine.
3. **Writer-first scripting languages with engine plugins.** inkle's Ink (80 Days: more than 600,000 words) and Yarn Spinner both have free Unreal plugins.

For NPC choreography, UE's Smart Objects plus StateTree is the built-in, data-driven path. Hard figures on person-weeks per hour of narrative for indie studios are largely unpublished.

### Cited Findings

**Valve dynamic dialog (Elan Ruskin, GDC 2012)**
- Talk: "AI-driven Dynamic Dialog through Fuzzy Pattern Matching. Empower Your Writers!" Valve's system uses "hundreds of facts about the world in a fuzzy pattern match against a database of thousands of possible lines" for responsive dialogue in Left 4 Dead and other titles. It can be extended to drive other game logic — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1015317/AI-driven-Dynamic-Dialog-through)
- Slide definition (from a search snippet; the PDF was too large to fetch): dynamic dialog is any system where characters speak and the speech seems to respond to players' actions and the world state — [Ruskin slides (Steam CDN PDF)](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2012/GDC2012_Ruskin_Elan_DynamicDialog.pdf)
- Matching rule: like Inform, it applies "the most specific one it can find," with less specific rules as fallbacks. Goal: make it "easy for the writers to author a lot of content." Example: a special quip for a character attacked in a circus versus elsewhere. Characters should "sensibly break off" banter when attacked. The result is "a strong illusion of situational awareness" with no planning AI — [Emily Short, 2012](https://emshort.blog/2012/03/16/gdc-2012-talk-on-dynamic-dialogue/)
- "Self-branching structure": similar world states produce similar but distinct responses without a hand-built flowchart. A Unity developer sketched a lightweight version: a PlotDirector holding world state in a dictionary, PlotRule assets, matched rules spawning PlotEvents. He expected about 1,000 rules and about 100 events for his project — [Robert Yang, Radiator blog, 2012](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2012/07/rule-databases-for-contextual-narrative.html)
- A 2013 Ruskin post (seen only in search summaries) says the system was used in Left 4 Dead, Dota and "basically all of Valve's games since The Orange Box" — [GDC Vault listing context](https://gdcvault.com/play/1015317/AI-driven-Dynamic-Dialog-through) (primary post not opened)

**Storylets and quality- or salience-based narrative**
- Quality-based narrative (Failbetter): content is cut into storylets unlocked by "qualities" (state variables). Short describes storylets as simple, atomic, robust and recombinable. Failbetter later dropped the "QBN" name. Salience-based narrative: the *system* picks the most applicable piece from a pool (Valve-style). In QBN, the *player* chooses among the currently legal pieces. Waypoint narrative: steering a conversation through a topic graph (her game *Glass*) — [Emily Short, "Beyond Branching" (2016)](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/); [Emily Short, "Storylets: You Want Them"](https://emshort.blog/tag/quality-based-narrative/)
- Yarn Spinner has built-in storylets and saliency (content tagged with conditions, best match selected) — [Yarn Spinner primer](https://docs.yarnspinner.dev/write-yarn-scripts/advanced-scripting/storylets-and-saliency-a-primer); academic map of the design space: [Kreminski & Wardrip-Fruin, "Sketching a Map of the Storylets Design Space"](https://mkremins.github.io/publications/Storylets_SketchingAMap.pdf)

**Ink (inkle) and Unreal plugins**
- Ink was built "for rapidly authoring branching text-based content in a writer-friendly environment" (GDC 2016) — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023221/Ink-The-Narrative-Scripting-Language). 80 Days' script is reported at over 600,000 words and 12,000 nodes — [Game Developer: open-sourcing ink](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/open-sourcing-80-days-narrative-scripting-language-ink) (figure from a search summary; not re-read)
- Heaven's Vault: dialogue "approaching 30,000 lines". A procedural layer pulls lines as the scene calls for them and falls back to generic topics when a character has nothing pointed to say. The procedural parts run inside Ink. inkle built automated testing into the writing workflow — [TechRaptor interview](https://techraptor.net/content/heavens-vault-joseph-humfrey-inkle-interview); [Emily Short on Heaven's Vault](https://emshort.blog/2019/07/23/heavens-vault-inkle/); ink 1.0 added a word-count and stats menu — [inkle blog](https://www.inklestudios.com/2021/02/22/ink-version-1.html)
- **Inkpot**: an open-source Ink wrapper for Unreal by The Chinese Room (made for Bloodlines 2 work). It targets UE 5.4+ and includes the "Blotter" runtime debugger for viewing and setting Ink variables, with full Ink List support in Blueprints. It needs .NET 5 for inklecate — [GitHub: The-Chinese-Room/Inkpot](https://github.com/The-Chinese-Room/Inkpot); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/inkles-scripting-language-ink-has-unreal-integration-thanks-to-bloodlines-2-developers-the-chinese-room/)
- **Yarn Spinner for Unreal**: pure C++, UE 5.4+, one-call save/load of all Yarn variables through USaveGame. Its status is alpha or beta depending on the page, and it is free — [Yarn Spinner docs](https://docs.yarnspinner.dev/yarn-spinner-for-unreal/unreal); [quickstart](https://docs.yarnspinner.dev/yarn-spinner-for-unreal/unreal/tutorial)

**UE Smart Objects and StateTree (NPC choreography)**
- Smart Objects are placed in a level and "contain no execution logic". They supply the information an interactor needs, and a reservation system manages them — [Epic docs: Smart Objects overview](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/smart-objects-in-unreal-engine---overview?lang=en-US)
- Common StateTree pattern: one state handles smart objects, with child states for reaching and using the object — [Epic docs: StateTree overview](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/overview-of-state-tree-in-unreal-engine)
- The Gameplay Interactions plugin combines Contextual Animation, Smart Objects and StateTree. As of Unreal Fest 2023 it was experimental, with docs lagging behind breaking changes — [UE forum, Unreal Fest 2023 talk](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/talks-and-demos-state-trees-and-smart-objects-data-driven-state-machine-workflows-for-open-world-ai-designs-unreal-fest-2023/1656473). Users report edge-case bugs, such as a StateTree on a Smart Object never firing (unverified forum reports).

**Prototype-first narrative production**
- What Remains of Edith Finch was built from 13 separate prototypes woven into one game (Ian Dallas, GDC 2018). Stories were in "rolling development," with one at the early-prototype stage at a time. The project began as a scuba-diving simulator — [Game Developer (GDC video)](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-weaving-13-prototypes-into-1-game-for-i-what-remains-of-edith-finch-i-); [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/sony-game-creator-tells-how-a-haunting-tale-came-about-and-is-still-being-rewritten/)
- Valve again: hiding the G-Man in shadow "reduced animation load and helped the schedule". Staging that cuts cost is a deliberate production lever — [HL:A commentary](https://combineoverwiki.net/wiki/Developer_commentary/Half-Life:_Alyx)

**Team sizes and timelines (the only hard numbers found)**

### Inferences
- **[inference] The core recommendation: one "Beat" pipeline, built once, used in all 17 chapters.** Combine the three proven patterns:
  1. **World Facts subsystem** (UGameInstanceSubsystem or UWorldSubsystem): `TMap<FName, float>` of facts such as altitude, O2, time of day, weather realm, companion distance, chapter, "SaidSeracWarning", fatigue and rope-clipped. This is the "hundreds of facts" side of Ruskin's design. Existing systems (body, weather, ropes) write facts. Lines and beats read them.
  2. **Rule-matched barks and radio lines** (Valve style): a DataTable or CSV of rows `{Speaker, Concept (e.g. "OnSlip", "OnSummitView", "Idle"), Criteria list, Line/sound, Cooldown, WriteBackFacts}`. The most specific matching rule wins, with fallbacks. "WriteBackFacts" gives memory, so the same warning is not said twice. A 1,000-rule table is small (the Radiator estimate). Matching cost is trivial if rules are bucketed by Concept.
  3. **Beat rows (storylet or set-piece templates)**: `{BeatId, Chapter, TriggerType (Volume / LookTrigger+Timeout / Fact threshold / Time), Preconditions (fact criteria), Actions[] (RadioConversation, CompanionGoTo SmartObject, AttentionDirector call, AudioLure, Beacon, Weather/Light change, Sequencer clip for NPC only), ExitFacts, Priority}`. Salience selection picks among eligible beats. Fixed story beats are simply beats with tight preconditions.
  4. **Conversations in Ink (Inkpot) or Yarn Spinner**: the writer authors branching radio and companion dialogue as plain text. The Beat system starts a knot or node, and Ink/Yarn variables mirror World Facts. Yarn Spinner has saliency and storylets built in, which suits barks. Ink has the larger shipped record (80 Days, Heaven's Vault) and an Unreal debugger (Blotter). Either way the writing is text, which an AI coding agent can read, lint, cross-check against facts, and turn into voice-line lists.
  5. **Companions through Smart Objects plus StateTree**: anchor points (rope anchor, belay stance, tent door, viewpoint) are Smart Objects. Companion StateTree states: Follow-on-rope, Wait-at-anchor, Lead, Perform-at-SmartObject (plays a montage or contextual anim), Talk-to-player (look-at plus Ink line). Per Alyx, design spaces so companions sit at a comfortable distance and never block the route. Per TLOU, keep the companion close so they never feel like a burden.
- **[inference] Why this cuts the "one week per chapter" cost:** After the one-time system build (estimate 3 to 5 weeks for an AI-assisted solo developer; my estimate, not a measured figure), a chapter becomes: (a) write the beats and lines as text, (b) run an editor Python script that places LookTriggers, volumes and Smart Objects along the route spline from a chapter beat sheet, (c) playtest and tune numbers. Steps (a) and (b) are where an AI coding agent is strongest: generating DataTable rows, cross-referencing fact names, writing placement scripts. The project already uses editor Python for bulk placement (camp props, rocks).
- **[inference] Greybox narrative first ("radio play pass").** Like Edith Finch's rolling prototypes: for each chapter, first ship a version where every beat is temp TTS or a text subtitle plus placeholder triggers, and play the whole chapter. Only then add audio, NPC animation and set-piece polish. That keeps the 14-hour arc testable early.
- **[inference] Automated checks** (after inkle's automated testing): a commandlet or Python test that loads every chapter's beats and checks that each precondition fact is written somewhere, each line has audio or TTS, and no beat is unreachable. AI agents are good at writing and maintaining exactly this kind of linter.
- **[opinion]** Use Valve-style rules for barks and reactive radio, and Ink or Yarn for authored conversations. Don't force one tool to do both.

### Gaps

## 4. How have AI coding assistants or procedural tools been used in 2024-2026 by small studios to produce narrative content, and with what honest results?

### Takeaway
The evidence base is thin, and opinion is sceptical, especially about LLMs generating dialogue *at runtime*. Use of generative AI is spreading (36% of surveyed developers use it personally), but sentiment worsened (30% see a negative impact, up from 12%). Respected narrative designers (Meghna Jayanth, Jon Ingold of inkle, Max Kreminski) call runtime LLM NPC chat boring, or say they barely use AI. No credible indie postmortem quantifying AI-written narrative was found. The defensible use for this project is offline: AI writes *systems, tools, data plumbing and checks*, while the developer (with AI drafting help if wanted) owns the words.

### Cited Findings
- GDC 2025 State of the Game Industry: 52% of respondents work at a company using generative AI and 36% use it personally. 30% say it harms the industry (up from 12%) and 13% say it helps (down from 21%). Concerns include IP theft, energy use, poor quality, bias and regulation. Narrative jobs grew from 14% to 17% of respondents but were hit hard by layoffs — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/gdc-2025-state-of-the-game-industry-devs-weigh-in-on-layoffs-ai-and-more)
- Meghna Jayanth (80 Days writer) called LLM chatbot NPCs in Where Winds Meet "horrible and boring" and saw no real place for them in fiction, except where the technology's failure is the theme. Max Kreminski (Cornell Tech) argued LLM chatbots won't tend to make good NPCs. He pointed to games that work by wrapping an LLM core in handcrafted interactions — [Rock Paper Shotgun](https://www.rockpapershotgun.com/horrible-boring-and-cheap-experts-pan-new-chatbot-npcs-but-some-leave-room-for-optimism)
- inkle's Jon Ingold had earlier called generative AI a "party trick". A year later he said he had barely used it, mainly for grammar fixes, and cited ethics concerns about training data — [Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/3d/video-game-design/how-indie-game-dev-opinions-on-generative-ai-have-changed)
- A small AI-tooling vendor reports that smaller models work "surprisingly well" for LLM NPC dialogue if the prompt is good. This is promotional and not independent — [DEV Community](https://dev.to/maximilian32541spec/how-we-built-dynamic-npc-dialogue-with-llms-lessons-from-early-access)
- Procedural (non-AI) narrative with a strong record: Heaven's Vault's procedural line selection inside Ink, and Valve's rule-matched dialogue — [Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2019/07/23/heavens-vault-inkle/); [GDC Vault, Ruskin](https://gdcvault.com/play/1015317/AI-driven-Dynamic-Dialog-through)

### Inferences
- **[inference]** For Yggdrasil's Peak, an AI coding agent's best return is in what section 3 lists: building the Beat, Facts, Rules and Attention systems in C++, generating and validating DataTable and Ink/Yarn content, writing editor Python that places triggers along the 1:1 route, and keeping a linter green. That is "procedural tools plus human-authored words," the combination with a shipped record.
- **[inference]** Runtime LLM dialogue for Nima or the Base Camp radio would conflict with the expert view above and add cost, latency and voice-acting problems. Not recommended for a thesis demo where tone (quiet awe, fate) matters.
- **[inference]** AI-drafted *variants* of barks (for example 5 phrasings of "rope's tight, move") written offline, then hand-edited and frozen into the rules table, sit between the extremes. Judging that is a writing-quality call for the developer, not a documented best practice.
- **[inference]** Honest risk: an AI agent speeds up systems and data, but the bottleneck can move to *playtesting and taste*. Edith Finch's rolling prototypes and Alyx's playtest-driven cuts both suggest that iteration with real players sets the schedule more than authoring speed does.
