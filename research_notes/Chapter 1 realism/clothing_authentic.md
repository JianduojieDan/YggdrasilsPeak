# Clothing: what the Chapter 1 cast really wears (Khumbu, late autumn, 2020s)

Research notes for *Chomolungma*, Chapter 1 (Dingboche, about 4,400 m, Oct/Nov). Written 2026-11-14.

**Confidence tags used below**
- **[V]** = stated by a cited text source (ethnographic, museum, news, outfitter).
- **[P]** = seen by me in a dated, located photo (listed in section 9). Treat it as one real example, not a rule.
- **[O]** = plausible from general knowledge or common practice, but I did **not** find a source that confirms it. Check it against photos before you rely on it.

---

## 0. The big picture (read this first)

1. **Sherpa men stopped wearing traditional dress day to day long ago.** A 1986 Wellcome Collection caption from Namche says "Sherpa men and boys frequently wear western clothes" while "the women and girls always dress in traditional Tibetan style" [V]. A 2008 Gale encyclopedia entry adds that traditional dress "is disappearing among men" and that younger men who have worked on expeditions wear Western high-altitude clothing [V]. So for Nima in 2020s camp, the right look is **outdoor gear, not a chuba**.
2. **Older Sherpa women in Khumbu villages still often wear the long dark wrap dress and striped apron at home and at work, with modern jackets, knit hats and sneakers over or with it.** Both of my Khumbu photo examples (Pangboche and Phurte, Oct 2009) show exactly this mix [P]. One Sherpa-authored source says women wear their cultural dress most of the time. A trekking-company source says it is mainly worn at festivals. Treat this as **varying by age and family**: the older the woman, the more likely she wears it daily [V, sources disagree].
3. **Girls and young women today mostly wear Western clothes in daily life** [O/P]. The 1986 "girls always traditional" statement is 40 years old. The young woman in a Namche homestay kitchen in 2009 wears a red fleece jacket and a ponytail [P]. Keep traditional dress for festivals: Losar, Dumji, weddings, and Mani Rimdu at Tengboche, which usually falls between mid-October and mid-November. That means it can fall **inside the Chapter 1 window** [V].
4. **Gear in Khumbu is a mix of real brands, gifts from clients, Nepali brands and counterfeits.** Thamel's supply of big-brand outdoor clothing is "virtually the entire supply... cheap counterfeits" (Switchback Travel) [V]. Climbing Sherpas often cannot afford name-brand gear, which is why the Nepali brand Everest Outfit was founded by two Sherpa guides [V]. Guides often get gear brought from abroad by clients or friends [V]. So a realistic local wardrobe is **mismatched, faded and a few seasons old**, never a matching new catalogue outfit.
5. **"Sherpa" is an ethnic group, not a job.** Porters on the EBC trail today are mostly from the lower eastern hills: Rai, Tamang, Magar, Kulung and others [V]. Do not dress or label every porter as a Sherpa.

### Weather that drives the layers (Dingboche, Oct/Nov)
- Namche (3,440 m) in October averages a low of 2 °C and a high of 12 °C (kimkim) [V]. Dingboche is about 1,000 m higher. Using the normal lapse rate, that gives roughly **−5 to −10 °C at dawn and about +3 to +8 °C in midday sun** in Oct, and colder in November. This is my estimate, so check it [O]. Another source gives autumn night lows at altitude of "−5 °C to −10 °C" [V].
- Sun is very strong. Outfitters require "good, dark, polarized sunglasses", a sun hat, a buff and maximum SPF (IMG 2022 Autumn EBC list) [V]. **Everyone outdoors at midday wears sunglasses.** Dawn and dusk mean down jackets for everyone.

## 2. (a) Nima: older Sherpa sirdar, in camp at about 4,300 m between expeditions

### What is documented
- Men wear Western clothing day to day (Wellcome 1986). Expedition men wear Western high-altitude clothing (Gale 2008) [V].
- Climbing Sherpas get gear from several places: foreign brands (The North Face, Mountain Hardwear named as too expensive), gear clients or friends bring from abroad, and Nepali brands such as **Everest Outfit**. Everest Outfit makes light down, sleeveless down (vests), hard-shell jackets and trousers, insulated trousers and hoodies, plus everyday t-shirts, trousers and stretch jeans. Kami Rita Sherpa is its ambassador (Onlinekhabar 2021; Everest Chronicle 2021) [V].
- **Sherpa Adventure Gear** (founded 2003 by Tashi Sherpa) sponsored Sherpa climbers to test its products and uses Sherpa/Tibetan textile patterns [V].
- **Blessing cords (sungdi / sungdü / sungkü):** a lama knots a cord, says a mantra over it and ties it **around the neck**. Climbers passing Pangboche got a "specially blessed and knotted gold string" around the neck from Lama Geshe (National Geographic) [V]. After a blessing at Tengboche (2025), RMI reports everyone got a khata and "a sungdi, which is a string we wear around our neck" [V]. Colours vary: white, red, yellow, green, blue (Raven Cypress Wood, a practitioner source) [V]. A sirdar who has been through many pujas and blessings plausibly wears **one or more thin, faded knotted cords** at the throat, often half-hidden under the collar [O, based on the practice].
- **Khata** (white silk scarf): given at blessings, greetings and farewells, and worn around the neck for that occasion [V: RMI]. Not a daily scarf.

### Recommended look (2020s, late autumn, camp)
- **Outer:** a mid-weight hooded **down jacket**, sun-faded and a few seasons old. Colour: dark (black, navy, charcoal) or an expedition colour (red, orange, yellow, royal blue). Small burn or patch marks are realistic, and so is a jacket from an expedition operator. No visible brand logos (project rule) [O].
- **Mid:** a fleece pullover or quarter-zip, or a wool sweater; optionally a **down vest** [V: Everest Outfit makes "sleeveless down"].
- **Legs:** synthetic trekking or softshell trousers (black, grey, olive, khaki). Track pants are also very common in camp [O].
- **Feet:** trekking shoes or approach shoes; in camp, possibly cheap sneakers, or down booties in the evening [O]. Not double mountaineering boots (those are for above base camp).
- **Head:** a knit wool or acrylic beanie, often with a Himalayan-style pattern, or a **baseball cap** in the sun [O].
- **Eyes:** **wraparound sport sunglasses or glacier glasses** [V: universal outfitter requirement], often pushed up on the cap or hanging on a cord [O].
- **Neck:** knotted **sungdi** cord(s) [V for the practice, O for him]. A buff or neck gaiter [V: outfitter lists].
- **Wrist:** a **mala** (Buddhist prayer beads) on the wrist or wound in the hand is very plausible for an older Buddhist man, but I found no source tying it to guides specifically [O]. A cheap **digital outdoor watch** is plausible. IMG asks clients for a watch with an alarm and light, which suggests the type [O]. Avoid luxury watches.
- **Kit:** a handheld radio clipped to a chest strap, a phone, a notebook. Sirdars run staff and logistics [O].
- **Face and body:** sun- and wind-darkened skin, cracked lips and sun-lines around the eyes from glasses fit a career guide [O]. (Check with `faces_realism.md`.)
- **Traditional dress:** only for weddings, Losar or formal ceremonies. A camp puja is done in everyday outdoor clothes [O].

## 3. (b) Aunt Pema: Sherpa woman, about 50, at home in a Khumbu village

### 3.1 Names of the garments (they vary, so they are flagged)
| Item | Name(s) | Notes / source |
|---|---|---|
| Long-sleeved floor-length wool dress | **tongkok** | Wikipedia (citing Lhakpa Norbu Sherpa, *Through a Sherpa Window*, 2008, pp.138–141); Khumbule [V] |
| Sleeveless version of the same dress | **angi / aangi / engi** | Worn over a blouse. Wikipedia and Khumbule say "in warmer weather". A Tibet-travel source says over a blouse in colder months, so **sources disagree** [V] |
| Long-sleeved blouse under the angi | **honju** (Wikipedia, from Lhakpa Norbu Sherpa); **wan-ju** (Ang Diku Sherpa); **raatuk** (blouse/under-layer, traditionally white raw silk *bure*) | "Honju" is **also** the Sikkimese Bhutia word for a blouse, so it is a shared Himalayan term [V]. Safe to use "honju" in game text |
| Front apron, striped | **pangden** (also *metil/matil*) | Wikipedia: pangden (or metil) in front [V] |
| Back apron, striped | **gyabtil / gyaptil / gewe** (Ang Diku also gives *matil* for the back) | **Names conflict** for which apron is "matil/metil". Use **pangden = front** and **gyabtil = back**, which most sources share [V] |
| Silver buckle holding the aprons | **kyetig** (embossed silver) | Wikipedia, Khumbule [V] |
| Waist sash | **kara** | Wikipedia, Ang Diku [V] |
| Women's festive hat | **shyamahu / shya-mu** | Gale; Ang Diku [V]. Festival only |
| Neck-to-chest gold/gem ornament / amulet box | **gau / ga'u** ("Kaou" in Ang Diku) | Gold, pearls, turquoise, coral, dzi, glass [V]. Festival or formal |
| Gold earrings | **mendok kogde** (Ang Diku) | [V, single source] |
| "Chuba" | The general Tibetan word for the wrap robe. Some sources call the Sherpa version **chhuba** or **bakhu** | Sherpa men's term in most sources [V] |

### 3.2 Who wears the front apron (pangden)? Front or back?
- **Sherpa-authored sources:** *"Both married and unmarried women wear the rear apron (Gyaptil or Matil), while the front apron (pangden) is worn only by married women"* (Ang Diku Sherpa, Musa Masala). Sherpa Adventure Gear: *"only married women can wear a front apron called a Pangden."* Gale (2008): married and unmarried women wear a rear apron, only married women a front apron [V].
- **Tibetan context:** commonly worn by married women, but some say it marks coming of age, and **unmarried girls now also wear it as part of costume**. It is associated mainly with U-Tsang (central Tibet), not Amdo or Kham. No historical record dates its origin (Tibet.net) [V].
- **SOAS (Fürer-Haimendorf slides, Khumjung, mid-20th c.):** women in festive dress wear "woollen robes, silk blouses and multi-coloured strip[ed] aprons". One young woman at Khumjung wears **front and back aprons**. Striped silk apron cloth was woven on a pedal loom in Kunde [V, catalogue captions only].
- **For Pema (~50):** if the script makes her **married**, give her **front pangden + back gyabtil**. If she is a **widow**, I found **no source** on whether widows keep the front apron. Flag this for a Sherpa consultant. Showing both aprons is the safer default for an older woman [O].
- **Look of the apron:** multicoloured **horizontal stripes**. Tibetan practice: rural women prefer strongly contrasting stripes and town women prefer fine stripes in similar shades (Tibet.net) [V]. The 2009 photos show **narrow, many-coloured stripes** in front of a dark skirt [P]. Some commercial pages say "red pangden". That is weak evidence, so do not make it plain red [V-weak].

### 3.3 Colours and fit of the dress
- The angi/tongkok on older women in my photos is **dark and plain** (black, very dark brown or charcoal), **ankle-length**, wrapped and belted [P]. Lonely Planet describes a woman "in a dark grey chupa" in Khumbu [V].
- Traditionally thick home-spun wool; **today mostly factory fabric** (Wikipedia: home-spun wool and silk are being replaced by factory-made material) [V].
- The blouse under it is often coloured or shiny (silk, satin, brocade or polyester). I could not confirm specific colours in a text source [O]. In cold weather it is usually hidden by a sweater or jacket anyway [P].

### 3.4 The everyday modern mix (what you actually see)
- **Pangboche, 21 Oct 2009** (senior woman carrying potatoes) [P]: bright **multicoloured headscarf** (pink/green), dark **patterned knit cardigan or jacket**, long dark skirt, narrow-striped apron, **white sneakers**, a **doko** basket on a **namlo strap running over the head** (on top of the scarf), and a wooden walking stick.
- **Phurte (Thame valley), 12 Oct 2009** (old woman) [P]: **cream knit hat**, **mauve/pink padded synthetic jacket**, long dark skirt, **striped front apron** visible.
- So for Pema: angi + aprons **plus** a fleece, cardigan, padded jacket or **down vest** on top, a knit hat or headscarf, and sneakers or trekking shoes. Indoors by the stove she may take the jacket off [P/O].
- A down jacket at home is realistic. A Solukhumbu woman recalls of her childhood home: "Our down jackets were our heaters" (Kathmandu Post 2023) [V].

### 3.5 Jewellery
- **Festive (not daily):** gold headbands, gold necklets, necklaces of **coral, turquoise, amber** and **dzi** beads, gold ear studs (SOAS captions, Khumjung) [V]. The big **gau** pendant [V].
- **Daily at home:** less. Small **gold earrings** and maybe a ring or one bead strand fit everyday life better. I have no text source for daily-wear amounts [O].

### 3.6 Things to avoid or flag
- **Nose ring:** a Commons photo labelled "Old Sherpa woman" (near Pakhepani, lower Solu, 2015) shows a large gold nose stud and pendant [P]. Nose ornaments are strongly linked to Hindu-influenced hill groups (Rai, Limbu, Gurung, Magar and others), and **no Sherpa dress source I found mentions nose rings**. The label may be loose, or the area is mixed. **Recommendation: no nose ring for Pema** (a Khumbu Sherpa) [O, flagged].
- **Traditional boots:** high wool boots with hide soles and red, maroon, green or blue uppers tied with coloured garters are described in older sources (Gale; Ang Diku) [V]. Today they are **festival or heritage items, not everyday**. Use sneakers or trekking shoes [P].
- **Hair:** long hair, braided or tied back, often under a knit hat or scarf [P/O]. I could **not** confirm a Sherpa-specific braid-with-red-yarn style. Do not copy the Kham/Amdo (Tibetan) red-tassel hairstyle without a source [O, flagged].

## 4. (c) Dolma: Sherpa girl, 8, at village home or school

- **Everyday 2020s:** Western children's clothes. Puffer or fleece jacket (pink, purple, red and turquoise are common children's colours [O]), track pants or leggings, a knit hat (pom-pom), sneakers or, in mud or snow, small rubber boots. Rosy, sun- and wind-chapped cheeks and dusty, worn clothes are realistic [O]. Evidence that girls have moved away from traditional dress since 1986: indirect only (the 2009 Namche photo of a young woman in a red fleece [P]). I found no study.
- **Traditional dress for a girl:** at festivals (Losar, Dumji, Mani Rimdu, weddings), a child-size **angi + blouse**. **Front apron = married-woman marker** in Sherpa sources, so leave it off or give a back apron only. Tibet.net notes unmarried girls sometimes wear it as costume [V]. Safest: no front pangden.
- **School:** Nepali government-school uniforms are typically a light-blue shirt with a dark-blue skirt (girls) or trousers (boys), but **each school picks its own** (histclo / general sources) [V-weak]. In Khumbu cold, children wear sweaters, track jackets or puffers over the uniform [O].
- **Where she would go to school (affects the story, unconfirmed):** Pangboche Basic School covers early childhood to Grade 5 (11 students in 2024/25, Edusanjal). Khumjung (Hillary) School runs pre-school to Grade 10 with 300+ students [V]. A search summary claimed **"There is no school here"** about Dingboche (ekantipur, Dec 2025), but I could **not** find that line on the page itself, so it is **unverified**. Many Khumbu families send children to boarding schools in Kathmandu [O]. Dashain/Tihar national school holidays fall around Oct/Nov, which would put a child at home [O].

## 5. (d) Porters on the EBC trail

### Who
- No longer mostly Sherpa. Kathmandu Post (2024) names porters from Sankhuwasabha, Khotang and Solukhumbu: Dahal, Magar, Tamang, Kulung [V]. Wikivoyage: guides are mostly Sherpa or Tamang, porters Rai or other groups [V]. Lowland porters "come from lower valleys" (Himalayan Journal 64) [V].

### Clothing and footwear: documented
- **Under-dressed for the cold, on a budget:** "most porters do not buy enough warm clothes"; some get by with one outfit all season (Kathmandu Post 2024) [V].
- **Thin clothing and poor shoes, including canvas shoes** (Kathmandu Post, Dec 2025, on porters generally) [V].
- **History:** until about 2000, many went high without gloves or boots, some in **flip-flop-type footwear** through snow. Frostbite was a recurring problem. Today **cheap imported trainers and jackets** are widely affordable, and good companies keep boots, jackets, trainers and hats in a Lukla store-room (Himalayan Journal 64, 2008) [V].
- **KEEP Porter Clothing Bank** (Thamel, since 2009) lends boots, warm jackets, gloves, hats and socks. Donated Western jackets and trousers **often don't fit the smaller Nepali porters** (KEEP) [V]. Expect **sleeves too long and jackets too big**.
- **Photo, Pheriche, 7 Nov 2022** (4,370 m, right next to Dingboche, in our season) [P]: **black puffy down jacket**, hood up over a **beige cloth** on the head, **face covered by a buff or mask**, **olive cargo trousers cut or rolled to mid-calf**, dark socks, **black low trainers**, and a wooden carrying frame with poles on his back.
- **Goldstar** is Nepal's iconic cheap, tough shoe brand (founded 1970; trainers, trekking shoes, sandals; widely faked) [V]. I found **no text source** linking it to porters, but cheap trainers of this kind are what you see [O].

### Load-carrying kit: documented
- **Namlo** = tumpline from the **forehead** to the load. **Doko** = conical bamboo basket on the back. **Tokma** = T-shaped stick to rest the load while standing; it also serves as a walking staff (Himalayan Journal 64; Kathmandu Post 2024) [V].
- **Loads:** commercial porters carry an average 89% of body weight, 20% of men over 125% (Basnyat & Schepens 2001). Trekking porters carry an industry limit of 30 kg, but in practice 35–40 kg (HJ 64). Kathmandu Post 2025 reports 40–60 kg bags against KEEP's 25 kg recommendation [V].
- **Trekking porters** usually carry clients' duffels (often two clients' bags) stacked and roped, wrapped in tarps or duffels, slung from the namlo, sometimes in a doko and sometimes without (photo: Namche suspension bridge, 27 Oct 2017, tall stack of yellow and blue bags [P]). **Commercial porters** carry lodge supplies (crates of drinks, gas cylinders, plywood) in a doko or on a frame. Plywood sheets roped on the back, Dingboche–Pangboche trail, Nov 2007 [P].

### Recommended porter variants (2020s)
1. **Trekking porter:** knock-off or donated puffer (black, navy, red), hoodie under it, track pants, cheap trainers, cap or beanie, buff over mouth (dust), sunglasses (cheap) or none, namlo over a folded cloth on the forehead, 2–3 duffels.
2. **Supply porter:** older fleece, rolled cargo trousers, canvas shoes or sandals with socks at lower camps, doko with crates, tokma in hand.
3. **Mixed-age:** include some older men and a few women (women porters exist, and NPR notes women in the study carried about 70% of body weight) [V].

## 6. (e) Expedition leader and clients, Dingboche to Lobuche, autumn

### The standard layering system (outfitter lists)
IMG 2022 Autumn EBC/Lobuche list [V]: 2 sets of synthetic long underwear; extra mid-layer (wool sweater, fleece or shelled vest); waterproof breathable shell jacket and trousers; **softshell trekking trousers**; a down or synthetic **parka big enough to go over everything**; light gloves plus heavy gloves or mittens; **warm wool or fleece hat**; **sun hat and bandana or buff**; buff or light balaclava against "Khumbu cough"; **watch** with alarm and light; **dark polarized sunglasses**; max-SPF sunscreen and lip balm; **medium-weight waterproof hiking boots** and gaiters; light shoes or sandals for camp; microspikes; trekking poles; 40–50 L daypack; porters and yaks carry duffels (no wheeled bags).
- **On the trail at midday:** base layer plus light fleece or softshell, sun hat or cap, buff, sunglasses, light gloves early, poles. **The down jacket lives in the daypack** and comes out at every stop, at dawn and in the afternoon wind [O, from the list logic].
- Plastic or double mountaineering boots are **only for the climb** (Lobuche peak), not for walking the trail [V: IMG lists them under "Lobuche Peak Climbers Add"].

### Marek Kovac (Slovak leader, 40s)
- An experienced Central European leader: **well-used, good technical gear**, consistent and muted (black, grey, dark green, navy) or brand-signature colours. Abrasion on the softshell, a sun-bleached cap, glacier glasses with side shields or wraparounds, a radio or satellite messenger, a GPS or altimeter watch [O].
- Slovakia's largest outdoor clothing maker is **Northfinder** (Polartec/PrimaLoft/Pertex fabrics; works with the Tatra Mountain Rescue Service) [V, self-reported]. European mountaineering brands are common among Slovak climbers [O]. Keep everything **logo-free** in-game.

### Elena Ruiz (Spanish first-time client)
- **Newer, mid-range kit**: some pieces still crisp, boots with little wear, a wide sun hat, lots of sunscreen and lip balm [O].
- Many trekkers **rent down jackets and down trousers in Thamel** for "less than a dollar per day". Rented pieces can be older and "light on down" (Switchback Travel) [V]. A slightly baggy, faded rented down jacket is a nice true detail.
- Thamel big-brand gear is almost all counterfeit [V], so a first-timer's "brand" fleece could be a copy.

### Ji-woo Han (Korean client who films everything)
- Korean hikers are known for **head-to-toe branded outdoor outfits**. AFP (2013): a $650 Gore-Tex jacket "will barely merit a second glance", with Korean brands Black Yak, K2 and Kolon Sport alongside The North Face. Korea Herald (2023): older Korean hikers' clothing is "vividly colored", and middle-aged men wore Gore-Tex and hiking boots in daily life long before "gorpcore" [V]. Major Korean outdoor brands: The North Face (Korea), Nepa, Black Yak, Kolon Sport, K2 (Asia Business Daily 2022) [V].
- **Colour:** a coordinated outfit, either vivid (older-generation style) or muted "gorpcore" (younger) [V]. Choose by age.
- **Sun protection:** Korean brands sell UV arm sleeves and UV face masks for hiking (K2 2026 product launch; Korean-market "climbing masks") [V for products]. A Korean trekker with **face cover or buff pulled up, a brimmed hat or visor and gloves** is plausible, but I found **no source on Korean trekkers in Nepal specifically** [O].
- **Filming kit:** action camera on a chest mount or pole, phone gimbal, power bank, spare batteries kept in an inner pocket (cold drains batteries) [O].

## 7. Common mistakes games and films make with Sherpa clothing

1. **Putting every Sherpa man in a chuba.** In reality men have worn Western outdoor clothing for decades (1986 Wellcome; Gale) [V].
2. **Dressing Sherpas as Tibetan nomads** (sheepskin chubas, fur trim, one sleeve hanging off, Kham or Amdo red hair tassels). The sleeve-off habit is a real Tibetan chuba practice (Wikipedia: Chuba) [V], but it is **not** what Khumbu Sherpas wear in a 2020s camp [O].
3. **Dressing them as lowland Nepalis** (saris, kurta, Dhaka-topi caps, nose rings). These belong to other Nepali groups [O].
4. **Front apron on girls or young unmarried women.** In Sherpa sources it marks a married woman [V].
5. **Festival jewellery as everyday wear.** Coral, turquoise, amber, dzi necklaces and gold headbands appear in **festive** dress photos (SOAS captions) [V].
6. **Spotless, matching, new outdoor kit on locals.** Real local gear is faded, mismatched, often a client's hand-me-down, Nepali-made or counterfeit [V].
7. **Porters drawn as all "Sherpas"**, or as either barefoot beggars or fully kitted pros. 2020s reality: cheap trainers, under-sized warmth, oversized donated jackets, buffs, namlo and doko [V/P].
8. **Wrong footwear.** Clients in double mountaineering boots on the trek, or Sherpas in yak-hide boots (heritage or festival only) [V].
9. **No sunglasses at altitude.** At 4,400 m everyone wears them outdoors around midday [V].
10. **Khata worn all day like a fashion scarf.** It is given at blessings, greetings and farewells [V].
11. **Using "Sherpa" as a job title** for every Nepali worker (Project Himalaya warns against "our sherpas") [V].
12. **Monk robes or ritual objects on lay villagers** [O].
13. **Real brand logos** (also a legal/IP issue for the game) [O].
