# Sherpa house and kitchen in the Khumbu: realism notes

Chapter 1 setting: a Sherpa family house near Dingboche (about 4,350-4,410 m), Khumbu, in late autumn (October/November), present day.
Compiled 2026-11-14 from web research. Everything below is paraphrased from the sources listed at the end. Each claim carries a source tag and a confidence note.

**How to read the tags**
- **[A]** primary or scholarly: UNESCO architectural survey, peer-reviewed papers, eyewitness ethnography, museum catalogue records.
- **[B]** journalism, NGO or museum-label material, archive photo captions.
- **[C]** travel, trekking or commercial pages. These are used only when nothing better was found, and are flagged.
- **EST** marks my own estimate. No source gives that number, so check it against reference photos before relying on it.

The two most useful sources by far:
1. **Sestini & Somigli, *Sherpa Architecture* (UNESCO, 1978).** Italian architects surveyed Khumbu houses in 1975 (Namche, Thame, Phortse, Khumjung and others), with plans and sections. [A]
2. **Charles Stonor, *The Sherpa and the Snowman* (1955).** Stonor lived in Sherpa homes in Namche, Khumjung and Thame in 1954 and describes the layout, the hearth and the copper vessels first-hand. [A, eyewitness]

Two modern correctives come from a 2023 survey of 223 buildings in the Thame valley (Frontiers in Built Environment) and a 2017 study of Khumbu wall materials (Mountain Research and Development).

## 0. Ten facts the art team must get right

1. **Shape and orientation.** The house is a long rectangle with a gable (ridge) roof. The back is dug into the slope, and every door and window is on the sunny downhill face (south or south-east). The back wall has no openings. [A: Sestini; Frontiers 2023; Wellcome 1986]
2. **Two storeys is the classic type.** Livestock and storage are on the ground floor (byre, fodder, firewood, dung) and the family lives upstairs. Single-storey buildings are also very common today: 73% of buildings surveyed in the Thame valley in the 2020s were single-storey, though that count includes barns and post-earthquake rebuilds. [A]
3. **Walls.** Thick rubble stone, usually dry-laid or set in clay mud, up to about 1 m thick (0.635 m measured in one Thame-valley house). Larger dressed blocks form the corners and the edges of openings. Walls were traditionally coated inside and out with clay mud, sometimes mixed with yak dung. A separate timber post-and-beam frame stands inside the stone shell and carries the floors and roof. [A]
4. **Roof today is corrugated iron.** In the Thame survey about 88% of buildings had corrugated galvanised iron (CGI) roofs and about 11% still had slate. The 2017 study found CGI on all three Khumbu building types it examined. The historic roofs were fir shingles or boards held down with stones, or stone slabs. [A]
5. **Windows are the colour accent.** Heavy wooden frames, often carved, are painted in vivid colours against grey-brown walls. Window panes were rice paper before glass arrived; by 1975 glass had largely replaced the paper. [A: Sestini]
6. **The upstairs is one long living room around the hearth.** A broad bench with a long low table runs under the windows. The owner's seat and the guest's seat of honour are beside the fire. The back wall, opposite the windows, carries long dark shelves of shining copper and brass water vessels. [A: Sestini; Stonor]
7. **The copper vessels are wealth on display.** Stonor says plainly that the number and size of the vessels showed a family's wealth and social standing. Rich houses held far more than they ever used. In the 1950s, expedition jerry-cans were collected for the same prestige. [A: Stonor; eHRAF summary calls the large copper kettles heirlooms]
8. **Smoke blackens the room.** Traditional hearths had no chimney: smoke left through a gap in the roof and left the rafters glossy black. Today many homes have a mud stove (Nepali *chulo*) or a sheet-metal stove with a pipe. Lodge dining rooms around Pheriche and Dingboche typically have a pot-belly stove burning yak dung, with a chimney. [A]
9. **Modern items are old news.** The 1975 survey already found a Chinese thermos flask, a pressure cooker and plastic knick-knacks next to the copper. Today solar lighting is normal in Dingboche (since at least 2014), plus LPG cylinders, kerosene and, in Namche, Khumjung and Thame, hydropower. [A/B]
10. **Dingboche specifics.** Historically a seasonal barley settlement. It now has about 50 houses, most of them converted to guesthouses. A realistic family house there is very likely also a small lodge, or right next to one. [B: Myrepublica, undated; Sestini 1975 names Pangboche as the highest village lived in year-round]

## 1. Dingboche and the setting

- **Seasonal origin.** In 1975 Sestini & Somigli described Pangboche, at nearly 4,000 m, as the highest village inhabited all year. Higher places served as subsidiary summer settlements, with huts as high as 4,750 m. Herders used high summer settlements (*yersa* or *phu*) and lower winter ones (*gunsa*). [A: Sestini]
  - A Sherpa-authored 2016 booklet (Tenzing Tashi Sherpa, published by the Khumbu Sherpa Culture Conservation Society and edited by Stan Stevens) lists the pasture names as *yarsha* (summer), *tyonsah* (autumn) and *gunsah* (winter). Private houses are built in these pastures; families without one use herding huts or rock-wall-and-tarpaulin shelters called *resa*. [A/B]
- **Dingboche today.** About 50 houses, most of them converted into guesthouses. [B: Myrepublica "And Dingboche gets deserted", undated, no author; treat as approximate]
- **The barley closure, *Dee*.** During the barley-growing period the village closes for roughly two months, from about the 6th to the 8th Sherpa month (summer, after the May trekking season). No one cooks in the village, so no smoke harms the barley. Nawa (village custodians) enforce the rule, and cattle are kept out. The village reopens when the crops are ready for harvest. [B: Myrepublica]
  - The Sherpa-authored booklet describes the same Nawa crop-custodian system and adds a rule: during the growing period, no freshly cut wood, leaves or forest litter may be brought into the village. [A/B: Tenzing Tashi Sherpa 2016]
  - By October/November the closure is long over and the harvest is in.
- **Fields.** Dingboche is known for small fields of barley and potatoes inside dry-stone walls. The walls shelter the crops from wind and keep grazing animals out. [C: several trekking sites, consistent with A: Sestini on Khumbu plots enclosed by low stone walls]
  - Sestini notes that field walls are built from stones cleared during land reclamation, often combined with turf sods. [A]
  - A 1986 Wellcome caption from Pangboche says potatoes are seldom grown above 4,000 m while barley grows higher up. [B]
  - Dingboche at about 4,400 m does grow potatoes today according to many recent sources [C], so treat the 1986 remark as dated or local.
- **Fuel at Dingboche.** In 2014 lodges imported kerosene and LPG by yak from Salleri. Juniper is protected. A lodge owner said lighting was no problem because it was already solar, while cooking and heating fuel was the cost problem. [B: Nepali Times, 2014-03-28]
  - The undated Myrepublica piece says residents rely heavily on firewood and that the Khumbu Alpine Conservation Council runs a kerosene depot in Dingboche. [B]
  - The 2016 Sherpa booklet says hotels and lodges now use electricity, gas and dry dung for fuel, and that building timber now comes from neighbouring districts rather than from Khumbu forests. [A/B]
- **Electricity.** The Amadablam mini hydro, built at Pangboche, was planned to serve 451 households in 19 settlements, Dingboche included, with completion targeted for early 2026. [B: Himalayan Times, 2024-10-01]
  - **Status in November 2026 unknown.** I did not confirm whether it has opened. For safety, show solar panels plus perhaps a power line; avoid rendering a dense electrical grid.

## 2. Exterior

### 2.1 Form, plan and storeys
- **Three types.** Sestini found three house types, all elongated, two-storeyed and ridge-roofed, with the ground floor partly built into the slope behind. [A]
  - The simplest single block is the most common.
  - Larger houses add one or two blocks in a line, or form an L. The L-plan is older and found only in some villages (Namche and Khumjung are named as exceptions).
  - In the larger houses the added upper room is a private chapel.
- **Orientation.** All houses in a village face the same way, south-east in the 1975 survey and south in Thame, to catch the sun. There are no openings at the back. [A: Sestini; Frontiers 2023; B: Wellcome 1986 Namche caption]
- **Yard.** A small open yard in front of each house is used for drying and sorting the harvest and for stacking winter firewood. [A: Sestini]
  - The 1986 Wellcome Namche photo also shows firewood stacked against walls. [B]
- **Storeys today.** The Thame-valley survey (223 buildings) found 73% single-storey, 25% two-storey and the rest mostly monasteries. [A: Frontiers 2023]
  - In 1954 Stonor reckoned about three-quarters of families had a two-storey house. [A]
  - **Conclusion:** a two-storey house is the traditional ideal. A single-storey kitchen house or annex is still realistic, especially for a modest or post-earthquake rebuild.
- **Measured example (Thame valley).** One traditional house in Hilajung was 15.5 m long by 5.8 m wide, with 0.635 m walls on both floors, a heavy slate roof and thick timber floors on joists and posts. [A: Frontiers 2023]
- **Ground floor height.** Stonor describes the byre as dark and barely high enough to stand in. [A]
  - EST: about 1.7-1.9 m clear.
- **Upper floor.** Reached by a wooden staircase that starts inside the byre, usually at the far right in Stonor's description. A short passage leads to the living room. [A: Stonor; Sestini]
  - A latrine sits on a partly roofed terrace or at the stair head, with leaf litter below that is later spread on the fields. [A]

### 2.2 Walls: dry stone or mortared?
- **Both are documented.**
  - Sestini 1975: stonework is either dry or bound with a rough mortar of soft clayey earth. [A]
  - Stonor 1954: thick, crudely hewn stone held together with mud mortar. [A]
  - Frontiers 2023 (Thame valley): mud mortar was traditionally rare in houses, and thick dry walls were the norm. 98% of rebuilt buildings had no mortar. The authors count a thin cement layer every second or third course as "no mortar". Cement mortar appeared in under 2%, mainly schools and monasteries. [A]
- **Thickness.** Often as much as 1 m (Sestini). Up to 1 m, with 0.635 m measured (Frontiers). [A]
  - EST for a modern rebuild: 0.45-0.6 m.
- **Corners and openings** are reinforced with large, squared, dressed stones. [A: Sestini]
- **Finish.** Walls are rarely left bare: inside and out they are plastered with clayey earth, which fills gaps and weatherproofs. [A: Sestini]
  - Frontiers says traditional walls were plastered with a mix of yak dung and mud. [A]
  - **For visuals:** a traditional house reads as mud-brown or grey-brown rendered stone, with stone showing where the plaster has weathered off. A newer house shows bare, neatly dressed, tightly fitted quarried stone.
- **Trend since 1975.** New houses use quarried, partly dressed stone with minimal joints instead of field or river stones, while still keeping a separate inner timber frame. [A: Sestini]
  - A 2015 post-quake trekker report says houses of rounded stones fared worst, while dressed and mortared houses did better. [C]
- **Timber frame.** Pillars carry three lengthwise beams. Joists are set on edge, with floor battens about 20 x 10 cm spaced 1.5-2 m apart and floorboards slotted into grooves. [A: Sestini]
  - Timber species: silver fir and birch (Frontiers); fir and pine from the Dudh Kosi forests (Sestini).
  - The roof frame has a raised ridge purlin plus two side beams, and rafters laid close together. In houses the eaves are simply the roofing projecting past the wall. [A: Sestini]
- **Earthquakes.** The 2015 earthquakes heavily damaged Thame. Out-of-plane failure of long thick dry walls is the typical damage. [A: Frontiers 2023; B: Thame Sherpa Fund]

### 2.3 Stone colour
- **Geology.** Granite and leucogranite are everywhere in the Khumbu: one UBC caption describes the building stone at Chhukung as leucogranite. [B: UBC 2020] A geology dissertation describes granite gneiss up to Namche and sillimanite-biotite gneiss from there up to Base Camp. [A: Wits]
  - "Leuco-" granite is light-coloured by definition: pale grey to off-white, often with dark mica or tourmaline specks. Gneiss is grey and banded. (Geological definitions, not Khumbu-specific observations.)
- **Visual guidance (EST, check against photos):**
  - Mid-to-light grey stone with grey-brown weathering, rusty iron staining in places, and pale-green, orange and black lichen patches on exposed faces.
  - Mud plaster in ochre-brown.
  - Older walls darken near the ground and under the eaves.

### 2.4 Windows
- **Size.**
  - 1954: two or three small windows with wooden shutters in the front wall. [A: Stonor]
  - 1975: large wooden windows on the valley-facing front are a prominent feature, finely carved with geometric designs. [A: Sestini]
  - Fig. 2 of Sestini (a house at Phortse): the living room is lit by three windows, and the private chapel by one large window.
  - Frontiers' improved design uses one door and four windows on the façade. [A]
  - Neither source gives window dimensions. EST:
    - traditional living-room window: 0.6-0.9 m wide x 0.7-1.1 m tall, sill about 0.6-0.9 m above the upper floor
    - modern lodge window: wider multi-pane glazing
- **Construction.**
  - A heavy frame (lintel, jambs, sill) is joined without nails. Tenons on the jambs lock into slots in the wall reveals. [A: Sestini]
  - A lattice counter-frame divides the opening into squares and oblongs.
  - A small projecting shoulder or hood over windows and doors gives weather protection.
- **Glazing.** Traditional white rice paper had largely been replaced by glass by 1975, though paper was still seen occasionally. [A: Sestini]
- **Colour.** Windows are painted in vivid colours that brighten the sombre walls; Sestini calls them visually the most important element. [A]
  - Sestini notes that monastery windows get painted stylised leaves and flowers on bright grounds against reddish-brown walls, which sets religious buildings apart from houses. House windows are bright but simpler.
- **Why blue or green?** I found **no source** giving a symbolic or traditional reason for blue or green frames on Khumbu houses.
  - The only documented rationale is aesthetic contrast (Sestini).
  - Treat any symbolic explanation, such as sky, water or Tibetan colour symbolism, as **unverified**. The choice may simply reflect taste and the paint available.
  - Multi-coloured frames (red, yellow, green, blue bands) and single-colour blue or green frames both appear in modern photos (EST, from photo impressions).
- **Not documented for Khumbu houses:** the wide black trapezoidal window surround typical of central-Tibetan and Ladakhi buildings. Do not add it unless a specific Khumbu reference photo shows it.

### 2.5 Doors
- Doors are on the front façade only. [A]
- Construction matches the windows: a heavy main frame built into the wall with a counter-frame. The leaf turns on wooden pins set into holes in the frame. [A: Sestini]
- A shoulder or overhang above the door carries one or more courses of stone slabs. [A: Sestini]
- The front door opens into the dark byre. Stonor calls it heavy. [A]
  - EST: about 0.9-1.1 m wide x 1.6-1.8 m tall, often with a high threshold.

### 2.6 Roofs: then and now
- **Historic.**
  - Wooden shingles, square or oblong and like those of the Italian Alps, laid along the slope and weighted with heavy stones. [A: Sestini]
  - Stone slabs, heavy enough to resist monsoon winds. [A: Sestini]
  - Bamboo matting weighted with stones as a ridge cap. [A: Sestini]
  - By 1975 boards had largely replaced slabs and shingles on new roofs. [A: Sestini]
  - Stonor 1954: a raftered roof with wood or stone tiles. [A]
  - Nepali Times photo caption, Namche 1976: fir shingles held down with stones, and at least five buildings already had corrugated metal roofs. [B]
  - eHRAF summary: wooden shingles. [A]
- **Pitch.** Ridge or gable, not the flat roof of Tibet. Sestini compares it to Bhutanese roofs. [A]
  - EST pitch: about 15-25 degrees.
- **Today.** CGI on about 88% of Thame-valley buildings and slate on about 11%. [A: Frontiers 2023] Primarily CGI across all three building types in the Sagarmatha National Park study. [A: Bhochhibhoya et al. 2017]
- **Roof colours.**
  - Khumjung is described as having uniformly green roofs. [C: travel guide]
  - A Getty caption mentions colourful corrugated roofs on teahouses. [C]
  - Elsewhere roofs are a mix of unpainted or rusting zinc, green, blue and red (EST, from photo impressions; no survey found).
- **Stones on tin roofs.** Commonly seen in photos holding sheets down against wind (EST; I found no written source). Stones on shingle roofs are firmly documented.

### 2.7 Prayer flags at the house
- **Poles.** Tall posts carrying Buddhist prayer banners are a feature of many Namche houses. [B: Wellcome 1986]
- **House rites.** In Sestini's account, monks go onto the roof and terraces during household rites and hang coloured prayer flags on long bamboo canes. [A]
- **Annual replacement.** A 2009 trip dispatch describes a family raising a new flag pole at their Khumbu house, with the annual replacement date set from the lama's calendar. [C: Berg Adventures]
- **Vocabulary.** The pole with a long cylindrical prayer banner is called *tarchen* (Sestini, describing the Tengboche courtyard). [A] The Sherpa word for the printed flags is *lung-ta*, "wind horse". [C]
- **Modern material.** Printed polyester flags are now common across the Himalaya (EST; a Sapiens article on this was not accessible).

### 2.8 Yard and surroundings (what to scatter)
- Firewood stacked against the house walls and in the yard. [A: Sestini; B: Wellcome 1986]
- **Yak and dzo dung (the main fuel up here).**
  - Above the treeline in Khumbu, yak-dung patties are used exclusively for heating and often for cooking. [A: Kanaan et al., Emergency Medicine, Dec 2016, reporting from Pheriche at 4,240 m]
  - **Drying on walls:** a Khumbu photo-gallery caption (Khumjung) describes people sticking yak dung on stone walls to dry. [C] The practice is well documented on the Tibetan plateau. [C] Treat it as plausible but secondary.
  - Patties are also dried on the ground and stored in heaps. The Namche Sherpa Culture Museum's reconstructed house keeps yak dung, wood and hay on the ground floor. [C]
  - A Pangboche student case study estimates 10-15 kg of dung per household per day. [C: Strathclyde student project 2016-17]
- Stone-walled potato and barley fields right up to the house. [A/C]
- A yard of flat stones or beaten earth in front of the door.
- Prayer-flag pole(s). Mani stones and chortens belong in the village, not usually in the yard.

## 3. Interior: the kitchen-living room

### 3.1 Layout (classic upstairs room)
Combined from Stonor 1954 and Sestini 1975 [A]:
- You climb from the byre, turn into a single large living room, and the plank wall divides off the stair.
- **Front wall (windows).** A broad bench runs along under the windows, with a long low table in front of it. People sit at floor or low-bench level. Chairs did not exist in 1954 (Stonor).
- **Hearth.** Placed near one end of the front (window) wall (Stonor). The owner's seat is beside it, with the guest's seat of honour next to that (Sestini). Family business is done around the hearth.
- **Back wall (opposite the windows).** Long, dark wooden shelves with rows of shining brass and copper water vessels and carved wooden bottles. Dressers and cupboards with sliding doors appear in Stonor.
- **Opposite side, between hearth and stair.** An alcove holds the bed of the housewife and the young children (Sestini).
- **Shelves by the hearth.** Cooking pots, teapots, crockery, the butter-tea churn, a wooden jug or cask of *chang* (barley beer) and, by 1975, a pressure cooker (Sestini).
- **At night** the family and guests sleep on carpets and brightly coloured blankets spread on the bench and floor (Sestini).
- **Well-to-do houses** add a planked-off room: either a spare bedroom and store (Stonor's Namche host) or a private chapel (see 3.6).
- **Partitions** are plank walls slotted into battens. Floors are carefully chosen boards, because life happens at floor level, and carpets are widespread (Sestini).
- **Mental images (two Namche houses, 1954, Stonor) [A]:**
  - Stonor's host family had two Tibetan looms crowding the living room, weaving scarlet wool rugs for sale.
  - A rich trader's house nearby had a painted prayer wheel in a corner of the living room that rang a bell each turn, and an elegantly frescoed chapel at the end.

### 3.2 Hearth and stove: what exists and when
| Era / building | What the fire looks like | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional house (1954-1975) | Open hearth, a square or raised box of stone slabs set into the floorboards near the window wall. No chimney: smoke escapes through a chink or opening in the roof, so rafters and roof boards are glossy black with soot. A pot always on the hob. Yak meat strips smoked on slats hung above the fire. | A: Stonor; Sestini |
| Same era, priest's house | A small smoky hearth in one corner with no windows; walls and raftered roof blackened by years of soot; copper pots, cupboards, dressers, a couple of benches and coarse rugs round the hearth. | A: Stonor |
| Pangboche homes (2016-17 study) | Nepali-named *agenu* (open fire) or *chulo* (mud stove), in small, poorly ventilated kitchens. Kerosene in the morning. Combined cooking and space heating. | C: Strathclyde student case study; treat with caution |
| Improved stoves (2004-05 onward) | SNV/WWF all-metal stove with a two-hole top and a chimney, designed for the Khumbu, to replace less efficient mud stoves. | B: SNV/Nienhuys reports (title and abstract only; PDF not readable) |
| Lodge dining rooms (2010s-now) | Pot-belly sheet-metal stove fuelled with yak dung, vented by a chimney, standing in the common dining area. | A: Kanaan et al. 2016 (Pheriche); B: Trailblazer guide (chimneys installed as lodges modernised) |
| Fuels now | Firewood (rationed by park and Nawa rules), yak dung, kerosene, LPG (refilling a cylinder cost NPR 12,500 in Khumjung, 2024), electricity where hydro exists, parabolic solar cookers. | B: Akshayakalpa 2024; Nepali Times 2014; Tenzing Tashi Sherpa 2016 |

**Terms**
- *Chulo* (चुलो) and *agenu* (अगेनु) are **Nepali** words for a stove and an open fireplace. [C/B]
- The Tibetan-family word for a stove or hearth is *thab*. Sestini gives the Tibetan kitchen-living room as *chakang* or *thapang*. [A]
- A Sherpa speaker would more likely say something like *thap* (with the stove in the room, *thap* or *thab*). I could not confirm the exact Sherpa form, so don't print it on-screen without checking with a Sherpa speaker.

**Visual guidance for a present-day Dingboche family kitchen**
- A clay or mud stove (or a sheet-metal box stove) with a stovepipe through the roof is the most defensible choice.
- Soot staining should be heaviest above and behind the stove, on the ceiling boards and on the beams.
- An open no-chimney hearth would also be authentic, but it belongs to an older or very traditional household.

### 3.3 Shelves of copper and brass: wealth on show
- **Wealth.** The back wall holds small iron cooking pots and great round copper water containers, some big enough to bathe in. Their number and size marked wealth and social prestige, and rich houses had more than they would ever use. Some were made locally; others came from Tibet or were bought in Kathmandu. [A: Stonor 1955]
  - Stonor describes a rich trader's house in Namche whose shelves held rows of great shining copper water vessels, which he calls the hallmark of social prestige. [A]
- **Expedition goods.** Stonor notes a craze for collecting expedition jerry-cans and metal containers to line the shelves for status. [A]
  - **A great period detail for Nima's house:** expedition-era containers next to family copper.
- **Heirlooms.** The eHRAF Sherpa summary calls the family's large copper kettles heirlooms. [A]
- **Brass in modern kitchens.** A 2025 visit to a Lukla Sherpa home describes brass and steel utensils lining the kitchen. [B: The National]
- **Named copper items** (International Mountain Museum, Pokhara labels, reproduced on wisdomlib; spellings follow the Nepali-script labels) [B]:
  - *meylang*: a copper "oven" to keep pots warm
  - *makal*: a copper fire container to keep a dish warm
  - *katir*: a copper vessel for kneading tsampa with butter and tea
  - *solok*: a copper container for cooked food
  - *thakya*: a tea stand with cup and cover
  - *shanshyu*: copper vessels for offering water to the deities
- **Colour.** Polished copper (orange-pink) and brass (yellow-gold) gleam against dark smoked wood. Older pieces carry dark patina and dents. Steel (Indian or Nepali stainless) and aluminium pots now sit alongside them.

### 3.4 Butter-tea churn (*dongmo* / *cha dong*)
- **Name.**
  - Tibetan *mdong mo*, pronounced roughly "dongmo". [B: Wikipedia, butter tea]
  - The V&A catalogues a Ladakhi tea churn as *cha dong* (tea churn). This is probably the "chandong" in our brief. Its exact Sherpa form is unverified.
  - The tea itself is *su chya* in Sherpa [B: Wikipedia]; written "su-cha" in The National and "suchya" in Nepali Times.
- **How it was used in a Khumbu home (1954).** Yak butter, salt and Chinese brick tea were churned with water in a tall brass-bound cylinder with a piston-headed plunger, then heated to boiling. A pot was always on the hob, and tsampa was often kneaded into the cup. [A: Stonor]
- **Dimensions.** V&A tea churn, Ladakh, 1960-80: wood with brass bands and an inner plunger, 82 cm tall, 13 cm diameter. [A: V&A catalogue]
  - Smaller bamboo churns exist; a dealer piece is 26 cm tall.
  - EST for a Sherpa household: 70-100 cm tall, 11-15 cm diameter, 2-4 brass or iron bands, plunger rod about 1.2x the barrel length.
- **Modern note.** Electric blenders are used for butter tea where electricity exists. [B: Wikipedia, general] Not verified specifically for Khumbu homes.

### 3.5 Benches, rugs and low tables
- **Seating.** A broad, low bench runs under the windows, with a long low table in front. [A: Sestini; Stonor describes the same window seat with a bench-table]
  - eHRAF: there is no furniture other than built-in platforms and benches for eating, sleeping and receiving guests. [A]
- **Rugs.**
  - Carpets are widely used on floor and bench (Sestini).
  - Stonor saw scarlet wool rugs with simple woven patterns being made on Tibetan looms in a Sherpa house. [A]
  - Use Tibetan-style pile rugs (*khaden* size, about 90 x 180 cm, EST) on the bench, with simpler, coarser rugs on the floor.
- **Low table (Tibetan *choktse* / *chokchi*).** Museum examples: LACMA about 41 x 79 x 33 cm (H x W x D); Rubin, mid-19th century, about 36 x 81 x 44 cm. [A]
  - Painted panels with floral or auspicious motifs are typical of Tibetan tables. A Sherpa farmhouse table may be plainer, planked and smoke-darkened (EST).
  - EST for the game: 30-40 cm high, 80-150 cm long, 35-50 cm deep.

### 3.6 Family shrine
- **Prayer room.** Every Sherpa house in Solukhumbu has a room or place for prayer, called *lhakhang* (*lha* = god, *khang* = house/room). [B: wisdomlib gallery text] Lamas and guests are received there for tea, and family members leaving on treks or climbs are given a *khata* there. [B]
  - **Relevant for Nima and for the hero's father's memory.**
- **Separate temple room (well-to-do families).** A square room with a large window and panelled walls painted with vivid religious scenes. The ceiling is divided into squares with inscribed mandala circles. [A: Sestini Fig. 2, a Phortse house]
  - Opposite the window: an altar with images of tutelary deities.
  - Either side: shelves of woodblock-printed books, water and offering cups, and butter lamps.
  - Painted scrolls (*thangka*) hang from the ceiling.
- **Modest houses.** The living room itself serves as the place of worship. Monks sit on the window bench to read texts during household rites. [A: Sestini]
- **Daily practice.**
  - 1974 photo of a small house chapel in Namche: brass and silver bowls on the altar filled with fresh water every day as an offering, and a poster of the King on the wall. [B: Peace Corps photo archive]
  - A Sherpa woman in an eastern-Nepal village lights a butter lamp before a photo of Guru Rinpoche each morning before cooking. [B: Nepali Times]
  - Modern equivalents: photos of the Dalai Lama, family photos draped with *khata*. The National describes a framed photo of a climber son with a *khata* in a Lukla kitchen. [B]
- **Cabinet.** The Tibetan altar cabinet is *chösham* (Tibetan མཆོད་བཤམ་): a painted wooden cabinet with niches for statues. [A: Rubin Museum]
- **Placement EST.** Back or end wall, away from the door, never at the foot of a bed. Seven water bowls in a row (common Tibetan Buddhist practice; not Khumbu-specific).

### 3.7 Water storage
- Great round copper water containers on the shelves, some large enough to bathe in. [A: Stonor] Rows of brass and copper water vessels on the wall opposite the windows. [A: Sestini]
- Water came from streams. Today plastic jerrycans and black plastic tanks are common (EST; not sourced).
- EST shapes: wide-bellied copper drums of 40-60 cm diameter on the floor or lowest shelf, plus smaller *gagri*-style necked pots (Nepali term).

### 3.8 Meat and cheese hanging
- Strips of yak meat are smoked on wooden slats hung from the roof above the hearth. [A: Sestini]
- **Yak/chauri hard cheese, *chhurpi*.** Described as one of the hardest cheeses in the world, made from chauri milk. [B: Akshayakalpa 2024]
  - Fresh cottage cheese is *sarkam* in a Sherpa village account. [B: Nepali Times]
  - Hanging *chhurpi* cubes threaded on strings near the fire is widely photographed (EST; not found in a scholarly source).
- **Late autumn is the classic time to slaughter and dry meat** (EST, from general Himalayan practice; not verified for Khumbu).

### 3.9 Light sources
- **Historic:** the hearth fire, butter lamps, small shuttered windows. [A]
  - Stonor mentions paraffin (kerosene), carried as Yeti repellent.
- **Kerosene lamps:** still the main lighting fuel for many Pangboche homes in a 2016-17 student study. [C]
  - Dingboche had solar lighting by 2014. [B]
- **Modern:** solar home systems with LED bulbs, solar lanterns, phone charging. Hydropower in Namche, Khumjung, Khunde and Thame since 1995 (Thame plant, Khumbu Bijuli Company). [B]
- **For the scene:** warm low firelight, one or two LED bulbs or a solar lantern, a butter lamp at the shrine, cold blue daylight from small south windows.

## 5. Late-autumn (October/November) state of the place
- **Fields.** Above 3,500 m there is a single crop cycle, roughly April to October. [B: ICIMOD economist quoted by Onlinekhabar] By late October and November, potato and barley fields are harvested, bare, brown and stony behind their walls (EST timing from that window).
- **Season.** Post-monsoon autumn is the main trekking and climbing season, so lodges are open and busy. Villagers are back from the summer pastures. [A: Tenzing Tashi Sherpa on seasonal moves; EST]
- **Stockpiles.** Winter firewood stacked in yards (Sestini) and dung heaps drying or stored. Prayer flags faded by the monsoon (EST).
- **Weather.** Cold mornings with frost on roofs. Snow possible but usually light at about 4,400 m in October/November (EST; check the climate notes from other topics).

## 7. Open questions and uncertainties
1. **Dingboche hydro.** Did the Amadablam mini hydro start supplying Dingboche in 2026? Unconfirmed. Use solar for safety.
2. **Paint colours.** No source explains blue or green trim, and no survey gives a distribution of roof colours. Rely on the user's reference photos.
3. **Sherpa-language terms** for stove, churn and table. Sources give Tibetan (*thab*, *mdong mo*, *choktse*) or Nepali (*chulo*, *agenu*, *gagri*) forms. The wisdomlib/International Mountain Museum labels call their terms "Nepali words" though they look Sherpa. **Check with a Sherpa speaker before any on-screen text.**
4. **Dung on walls in Dingboche specifically.** Plausible but only weakly sourced for Khumbu (a single Khumjung photo caption).
5. **Window and door dimensions.** Not given in readable sources. The plans in Sestini Figs. 2 and 14 and Frontiers Fig. 2 carry the drawings but were not machine-readable. Measuring those figures would firm up the numbers.
6. **Interior wall finish in modern houses.** Painted plaster or plywood panelling is common in lodges (EST). No source found.
7. **Rice in yard drying.** Sestini mentions drying rice, buckwheat and millet in house yards. Rice does not grow in Khumbu, so this is probably a generalisation; for Dingboche use barley and potatoes.
8. **The 1986 Wellcome caption** (potatoes seldom above 4,000 m) conflicts with modern Dingboche potato growing. Likely dated or local.
